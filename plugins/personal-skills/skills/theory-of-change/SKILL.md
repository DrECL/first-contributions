# Theory of Change

A Theory of Change (ToC) explains *how and why* a desired change is expected to happen in a particular context. It is not a task list — it is a causal map from long-term impact backward to the concrete work, made explicit enough that others can test it, fund it, and evaluate it.

This skill facilitates that map. Work through it collaboratively with the user; do not generate a finished ToC in one shot. At each step, reflect their input back, name the assumptions, and confirm before moving on.

## Apply the hope-based frame throughout
Load `C:\Users\macro\.claude\brand\hope-based-framework.md` when building for Macro Minded. Frame from possibility, not deficit: describe the future being built toward, the strengths of the people and systems involved, and systems as *ready for transformation* rather than broken. State problems as gaps between current and possible, not as pathology.

## The method: map backward, plan forward
ToC is built in reverse of how it's delivered. Always start at the end.

### Step 1 — Name the long-term impact
The ultimate change you exist to create. Big, values-level, often beyond any single program's sole control. Ask: *If this work fully succeeded over years, what would be different in the world/community/system?*

### Step 2 — Define the ceiling of accountability
Draw the line between what this effort can be *held accountable* for (its sphere of control and influence) and the broader impact it *contributes* to but cannot guarantee alone. This honesty is what makes a ToC credible to funders and evaluators.

### Step 3 — Backwards-map the outcomes pathway
From the impact, ask repeatedly: *What must be true first for this to happen?* Each answer is a **precondition/outcome**. Keep asking down the chain until you reach outcomes the work can directly produce. Result: a stacked pathway of long-term → intermediate → early outcomes, each causally linked to the one above it.

### Step 4 — Surface assumptions at every link
For each arrow between outcomes, state the assumption that makes it hold ("we assume that if X, then Y, because..."). These are where theories of change usually fail. Distinguish:
- **Contextual assumptions** — about the environment, populations, systems
- **Causal assumptions** — about why one outcome leads to the next
- **Evidence base** — research, prior results, or practice wisdom that supports the link (provide attribution for all sources via active hyperlinks as applicable)

### Step 5 — Identify interventions/strategies
For the outcomes the work directly produces, name the concrete strategies, activities, and inputs that drive them. This is the only forward-facing part — and it comes last, so activities serve the pathway rather than the pathway being reverse-engineered to justify activities.

### Step 6 — Set indicators
For each outcome, define how you'd know it's happening: an observable indicator, who/what it measures, and a rough target or direction. Keep them meaningful, not just countable.

### Step 7 — Write the narrative
Produce a short prose narrative (the "change story") that walks the pathway from strategies up to impact, weaving in the key assumptions. This is what goes in a grant proposal or strategy doc — the diagram supports it, the narrative carries it.

## Output
Deliver, in order:
1. **Impact statement** + ceiling of accountability
2. **Outcomes pathway** (long-term → intermediate → early), shown as a nested list or simple text diagram
3. **Assumptions register** — one line per causal link, with evidence flagged
4. **Strategies & inputs** mapped to the outcomes they drive
5. **Indicators table** — Outcome | Indicator | Measures | Target/direction
6. **Change narrative** — 150–300 words in the user's voice

Save to the project's planning folder, or ask where. If it's for a grant, mirror the funder's language for outcomes where possible.

## Quality checks
- [ ] Built backward from impact, not forward from activities
- [ ] Every causal link has a stated assumption
- [ ] Weak links (hope, no evidence) are flagged honestly, not hidden
- [ ] Ceiling of accountability is explicit
- [ ] Indicators are meaningful, not vanity metrics
- [ ] Hope-based framing: possibility-first, strength-based, systems-ready-for-change
- [ ] All sources are fact-checked for accuracy and documented in the output
- [ ] The narrative would make sense to a funder who's never met the program
